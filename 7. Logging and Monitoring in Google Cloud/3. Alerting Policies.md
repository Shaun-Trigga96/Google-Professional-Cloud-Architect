# Understanding SLI, SLO, and SLA

## Key Terms

### Service Level Indicator (SLI)

- **Definition**: Carefully selected monitoring metrics that measure one aspect of a service's reliability.
- **Expression**: Typically expressed as the ratio of good events to all valid events.
- **Example**: Error rate, response time.

### Service Level Objective (SLO)

- **Definition**: Combines an SLI with a target reliability.
- **Target**: Generally just short of 100% (e.g., 99.9% or "three nines").
- **SMART Criteria**:
  - **Specific**: Clearly defined (e.g., "95th percentile of results are returned in under 100ms").
  - **Measurable**: Based on quantifiable indicators.
  - **Achievable**: Realistic targets (e.g., "100% Availability" is not achievable).
  - **Relevant**: Important to the user and application goals.
  - **Time-bound**: Defined time period (e.g., per year, per month).

### Service Level Agreement (SLA)

- **Definition**: Commitments made to customers about the minimum levels of service and the consequences of not meeting those levels.
- **Compensation**: May include refunds or credits for outages longer than allowed.
- **Alerting Thresholds**: Often set higher than SLA minimums to detect problems early.

## Importance of SLI, SLO, and SLA

- **Agreement**: All parts of the business must agree on these metrics as accurate measures of user experience.
- **Consequences**: Breaching SLOs and SLAs must have well-documented consequences.
- **Example**: An SLA to maintain an error rate of less than 0.3% for the billing system.
  - **SLI**: Error rate.
  - **SLO**: 0.3% target.

## Practical Application

- **Internal Targets**: Set higher alerting thresholds than SLA minimums to detect and address issues before they impact customers.
- **Operational Response**: Monitoring systems should trigger responses based on these internal targets.
- **Executive Support**: Necessary to enforce consequences and drive changes in development practices.

## Example

- **SLA Example**: Maintain an error rate of less than 0.3% for the billing system.
  - **SLI**: Error rate.
  - **SLO**: 0.3% target.
  - **SLA**: Compensation for outages beyond the agreed threshold.

### Challenges with SLAs

- **Minimum Service Levels**: SLAs often incentivize only the minimum service levels to avoid customer churn.
- **Customer Perception**: Reliability issues can affect customer perception before SLA breaches occur.
- **Cost of Compensation**: Frequent compensation can be expensive, highlighting the need for higher internal targets.

### Alerting Strategy

- **Higher Thresholds**: Set alerting thresholds higher than SLA minimums to allow for proactive problem resolution.
- **Operational Response**: Ensure monitoring systems trigger timely operational responses to maintain service reliability.

# Developing an Alerting Strategy

## Understanding Alerts

- **Definition**: An alert is an automated notification sent by Google Cloud through a notification channel to an external application, ticketing system, or person.
- **Purpose**: Alerts are generated when something needs to change, such as a service being down or an SLO not being met.

## Time Series and Error Budget

- **Time Series**: A series of event data points broken into successive, equally spaced windows of time.
- **Error Budget**: Defined as perfection minus SLO. For example, if the SLO target is "90% of requests must return in 200 ms," the error budget is 100% - 90% = 10%.

## Attributes of an Effective Alerting Strategy

1. **Precision**:
   - Proportion of relevant alerts to the sum of relevant and irrelevant alerts.
   - Decreased by false alerts.
   - Measure of exactness.

2. **Recall**:
   - Proportion of relevant alerts to the sum of relevant alerts and missed alerts.
   - Decreased by missing alerts.
   - Measure of completeness.

3. **Detection Time**:
   - Time taken to notice an alert condition.
   - Long detection times can negatively affect the error budget, but alerting too fast can generate false positives.

4. **Reset Time**:
   - Time taken for alerts to stop firing after an issue is resolved.
   - Continued alerts on repaired systems can lead to confusion.

## Window Length for Alerts

- **Short Windows**:
  - Yield faster alert detections and shorter reset times.
  - Tend to decrease precision due to false positives.
  - Example: A 10-minute window in a 99.9% availability SLO over 30 days would alert in 0.6 seconds if a full outage occurs, consuming only 0.02% of the error budget.

- **Long Windows**:
  - Yield better precision but longer reset and detection times.
  - Example: A 36-hour window in the same SLO would alert in 2 minutes and 10 seconds, consuming 5% of the error budget.

- **Successive Failure Count**:
  - Use short windows but add a successive failure count to improve precision.
  - Example: An alert triggers if three consecutive windows fail.

## Balancing Precision and Recall

- **Inverse Relationship**: As precision increases, recall may decrease, and vice versa.
- **Multiple Conditions**: Define multiple conditions in an alerting policy to improve precision, recall, detection time, and reset time.

## Alerting Channels and Severity Levels

- **Multiple Channels**: Define multiple alerts through multiple channels for redundancy.
  - High-priority alerts: Slack, SMS, PagerDuty.
  - Low-priority alerts: Logged, email, support ticket management system.

- **Severity Levels**:
  - Use severity levels to prioritize alerts based on customer impact and SLA.
  - Custom severity levels can be included in notifications for effective alerting and integration with third-party services.

## Example Alerting Strategy

- **Short Window Condition**: Generates an alert as a Pub/Sub message to a Cloud Run container, which uses complex logic to check multiple conditions before notifying a human.
- **Prioritization**: Alerts should be prioritized based on customer impact and SLA, involving humans only for critical alerts.

By following these guidelines, you can develop an effective alerting strategy that balances precision, recall, detection time, and reset time, ensuring timely and accurate notifications for your cloud applications.

# Creating Alerts in Google Cloud

## Alerting Policies

- **Components of an Alerting Policy**:
  - **Name**: Use a descriptive name for easy recognition.
  - **Alert Conditions**: Define what is being monitored and under what conditions an alert should be generated.
  - **Notifications**: Specify how alerts are sent to recipients.
  - **Documentation**: Provide additional information to help troubleshoot the alert.

- **Creating Alert Policies**:
  - Can be created using the Google Cloud console, gcloud CLI, API, or Terraform.
  - Start with an alert policy definition in JSON or YAML format.
  - Use `gcloud monitoring policies list` and `describe` commands to see the definition file.

## Types of Alerting Policies

1. **Metric-Based Alerting**:
   - Track metric data collected by Cloud Monitoring.
   - Example: Notify when a VM has high latency for a significant time period.

2. **Log-Based Alerting**:
   - Notify anytime a specific message occurs in a log.
   - Example: Notify when a human user accesses the security key of a service account.

## Configuring Alert Conditions

- **Target Resource and Metric**: Start with the resource and metric to monitor.
- **Filter, Group By, and Aggregate**: Customize the measure you require.
- **Trigger Condition, Threshold, and Duration**: Configure the logic for triggering the alert.

### Types of Conditions for Metric-Based Alerts

1. **Metric-Threshold Conditions**: Trigger when metric values exceed a threshold for a specific duration.
2. **Metric-Absence Conditions**: Trigger when there is an absence of measurements for a duration window.
3. **Forecast Conditions**: Predict future behavior based on previous data and trigger when a threshold is predicted to be violated.

## Notification Channels

- **Direct-to-Human**: Email, SMS, Slack, Mobile Push.
- **Third-Party Integration**: Webhook, Pub/Sub.
- **User-Defined Labels**: Include labels to indicate severity and help prioritize alerts.

## Documentation

- **Purpose**: Provide additional information to help troubleshoot the alert.
- **Content**: Include internal playbooks, landing links, and dynamic labels.
- **Example**: Reference standard solutions or automate if possible.

## Managing Incidents

- **Summary of Incidents and Alerting Events**:
  - **Event**: Occurs when alerting policy conditions are met.
  - **Incident**: Opened by Cloud Monitoring when an event occurs.

- **Incident States**:
  - **Incident Firing**: Conditions are being met or no data indicates otherwise.
  - **Acknowledged Incidents**: Marked by a technician as being dealt with.
  - **Alert Policies**: Displays the number of alerting policies created.

- **Viewing Incidents**:
  - **Show Closed Incidents**: List the most recent incidents, including closed ones.
  - **Snooze**: Temporarily prevent alerts from being created and notifications from being sent.

By following these guidelines, you can effectively create and manage alerts in Google Cloud, ensuring timely notifications and efficient incident management.

## Snooze Functionality

- **Purpose**: Temporarily prevent alerts from being created and notifications from being sent.
- **Use Case**: During an escalating outage to reduce the number of new notifications.

## Groups and Subgroups

- **Purpose**: Alert on the behavior of a set of resources instead of individual resources.
- **Example**: Create an alerting policy triggered if some resources in the group violate a condition (e.g., CPU load).
- **Structure**: Groups can contain subgroups and can be up to six levels deep.
- **Applications**:
  - Separate monitoring of production resources from test or development resources.
  - Monitor production resources by zone.
- **Membership Criteria**: Based on resource name or type, Cloud Projects, network tag, resource label, security group, region, or App Engine app or service.

## Logs-Based Metrics

- **Definition**: Extracted from Cloud Monitoring based on the content of log entries.
- **Examples**:
  - Record the number of log entries containing particular messages.
  - Extract latency information reported in log entries.
- **Usage**: Can be used in Cloud Monitoring charts and alerting policies.

## Creating Alerting Policies with Terraform

- **Description**: Define conditions under which some aspect of your system is considered "unhealthy" and ways to notify people or services.
- **Basic Monitoring Alert Policy**:
  - **Display_name**: Identifies the policy with a name visible on the dashboard.
  - **Combiner**: Defines how the results of multiple conditions are combined.
  - **Conditions**: Defines a list of conditions combined based on the combiner.
  - **Limit**: A policy can have up to six conditions.

### Example Code Structure

```hcl
resource "google_monitoring_alert_policy" "alert_policy" {
  display_name = "Example Alert Policy"
  combiner     = "AND"
  conditions {
    condition_threshold {
      filter     = "metric.type=\"compute.googleapis.com/instance/cpu/utilization\""
      comparison = "COMPARISON_GT"
      threshold_value = 0.8
      duration   = "60s"
    }
  }
}
```

For more policy examples, refer to the official documentation.

## Summary

- **Alerting Policies**: Define what is monitored and when to trigger an alert.
- **Notification Channels**: Specify how alerts are sent (Email, SMS, Slack, Webhook, Pub/Sub).
- **Documentation**: Provide additional information to help troubleshoot the alert.
- **Incident Management**: Monitor and manage incidents through the alerting web interface.

# Service Monitoring in Google Cloud

## Overview

Modern applications are composed of multiple interconnected services. When something fails, it often affects multiple services simultaneously. Service Monitoring helps manage this complexity by assisting with SLO and alert creation.

### Key Questions Answered by Service Monitoring

1. **What are your services?**
2. **What functionality do those services expose to internal and external customers?**
3. **What are your promises and commitments regarding the availability and performance of those services, and are your services meeting them?**
4. **For microservices-based apps, what are the inter-service dependencies?**
5. **How can you use that knowledge to double-check new code rollouts and triage problems if a service degradation occurs?**
6. **Can you look at all the monitoring signals for a service holistically to reduce mean time to repair (MTTR)?**

## Service Monitoring Features

- **Service Types**: GKE namespaces, GKE services, GKE workloads, Cloud Run services.
- **Services Overview Page**: Provides a summary of the health of your various services, including service name, type, SLO status, and any SLO-related alerts.

## SLO Compliance Calculations

1. **Request-Based SLOs**:
   - Use a ratio of good requests to total requests.
   - Example: 95% of requests must have a latency below 100 ms.

2. **Window-Based SLOs**:
   - Use a ratio of the number of good versus bad measurement intervals (windows).
   - Example: 99% of 10-minute windows must have 95% of requests with latency below 100 ms.

### Example Comparison

- **Request-Based SLO**: 1,000,000 requests/month with 99.9% compliance allows 1,000 bad requests.
- **Window-Based SLO**: 99.9% compliance over 1-minute windows allows 43 bad windows out of 43,200 total windows.

## Creating SLOs with Service Monitoring

1. **Select a Service**: On the Services overview page, select a listed service.
2. **Create SLO**: Click **Create SLO**.
3. **Select SLI Metric**: Options include availability, latency, and other (custom SLI).
4. **Choose SLO Type**: Request-based or window-based.
5. **Set Compliance Period**: Choose calendar-based or rolling period.
6. **Set Performance Goal**: Enter a percentage to set the performance target for the SLI.
7. **Create Alerting Policy**: Click **Create alerting policy**.

## Monitoring and Alerts

- **Service Details**: View existing SLOs, SLI status, error budget remaining, and SLO compliance.
- **Alerting**: Trigger alerts when a service is heading to violate an SLO using a lookback window and burn rate threshold.

### Example Alerting Policy

- **Lookback Window**: 60 minutes.
- **Burn Rate Threshold**: 1 (uses 100% of the error budget by the end of the 7-day period).
- **Alert Trigger**: If the error budget is projected to be exhausted in 1/10th of the 7-day period or faster.

## Monitoring SLOs

- **SLI Status**: Current SLI values.
- **Error Budget**: Remaining error budget.
- **Compliance**: Current level of SLO compliance.
- **Alerts**: Status of any set alerts.

   - Close the tab, refresh the Notification Channels, select your Display name, and click **OK**.
   - Click **Next**, skip the optional steps, and click **Save**.

4. **Trigger the Alert**:
   - Modify the application to trigger the alert.
   - In the Cloud Shell code editor, open `index.js`.
   - Scroll to the `/random-error` route and modify the value next to `Math.random` from 1000 to 20.
   - Close the editor and switch to the terminal window.
   - Redeploy the change to App Engine:

     ```bash
     gcloud app deploy
     ```

   - When prompted, type `y` and press Enter.
   - Verify the uptick in errors in the tab running the test loop.
   - Switch back to the Service Monitoring page and ensure **Auto Refresh** is enabled.
   - Verify the SLO is expanded and you can see the Service level indicator.
   - After a few minutes, the SLI value and chart should show a decrease in performance to about 95%. You should also receive the alert notification email.

### Note

You may see your error budget quickly drop disproportionately due to the small dataset. If you fix the problem, the error budget will rapidly fill back up, though it might take a couple of days to show accurately.

By following these steps, you will create an availability SLO and corresponding alert, and test the alert by modifying the application to trigger it.
