# Defining Services

## Introduction

A new development begins with the planning and design phases. These require information gathering, starting with business requirements. Once the requirements are defined, it is important to measure that they're providing business value.

## Module Overview

In this module, we will look at gathering requirements and then techniques for measuring the impact of these solutions.

### Key Topics

- **Describing Users**: Describe users of a system in terms of the roles and personas they take. These users will help define and refine the qualitative requirements, which will be captured in the form of user stories. These provide a context for the architectural design and subsequent technical decisions you will make as a Cloud architect.

- **Business Requirements**: Examples include accelerating the pace of software development, reducing capital expenditure, and reducing time to recover incidents.

- **Technical Requirements**: The functional and non-functional features required for the system.

- **Measuring Success**: Learn how to measure success using key performance indicators (KPIs).

- **SMART Criteria**: Discuss the importance of using SMART criteria when defining KPIs.

- **Service Level Objectives (SLOs) and Service Level Indicators (SLIs)**: Consider the most suitable SLOs and SLIs, and from these, service level agreements (SLAs).

# Requirements, Analysis, and Design

## Key Questions for Building Requirements

Useful questions to ask a cloud architect to help build the requirements are: Who, what, why, when, and how?

- **Who**: Determine not only the user of the system, but also the developers and stakeholders. Aim to build a full picture of who the system will affect both directly and indirectly.
- **What**: Establish the main areas of functionality required in a clear, unambiguous manner.
- **Why**: Understand the problem the proposed system aims to address or solve. This helps in defining KPIs, SLOs, and SLAs.
- **When**: Determine a realistic timeline and help contain the scope.
- **How**: Determine non-functional requirements such as user concurrency, average payload size, latency requirements, and geographical distribution of users.

## Defining User Roles

In the previous design activity, you defined user roles for your application. Roles represent the goal of a user at some point and enable the analysis of a requirement in a particular context. A role is not necessarily a person; it can be an actor on the system, such as a microservice client accessing another microservice.

### Process for Determining Roles

1. **Brainstorm Initial Set**: Write as many roles as you can think of, each representing a single user.
2. **Organize**: Identify overlapping and related roles and group them together.
3. **Consolidate**: Remove duplication and condense the roles.
4. **Refine**: Include internal and external roles, different user patterns, and additional information such as the user's level of expertise and frequency of use.

## Creating Personas

A persona is an imaginary representation of a user role. It helps architects and developers think about the characteristics of users by personalizing them. Often, a role has multiple personas.

### Example

For a banking application:

- **Persona**: Jocelyn, a busy working mom who wants to save time and money, perform standard banking operations online, and receive benefits such as cash back.

Using personas helps build a fuller picture of the requirements and can guide design decisions.

## Writing User Stories

User stories describe one thing a user wants the system to do. They are written in a structured way, typically using the form:

- "As a [type of user], I want to [do something] so that I can [get some benefit]."
- Another form: "Given [some context], when I [do something], then [this should happen]."

### Example

For a banking system:

- **Title**: Balance Inquiry
- **Story**: "As an account holder, I want to check my available balance at any time of day so I am sure not to overdraw my account."

## Evaluating User Stories with INVEST Criteria

- **Independent**: A story should be independent to prevent problems with prioritization and planning.
- **Negotiable**: Stories are not written contracts but stimulate discussion between customers and developers until there is a clear agreement.
- **Valuable**: Stories should provide value to users, focusing on outcomes and impacts.
- **Estimatable**: The story must be estimatable, indicating that details are sufficient and the story is not too large.
- **Small**: Good stories should be small to keep scope manageable and support fast feedback.
- **Testable**: Stories must be testable so developers can verify implementation and validate requirements.

# Measuring Technical and Business Requirements

## Introduction

With a set of requirements in place, we will now consider how to measure whether the technical and business requirements have been met. To manage a service well, it is important to understand which behaviors matter, and how to measure and evaluate these behaviors within the context of constraints like time, funding, and people.

## Key Metrics for Different Systems

### User-Facing Systems

- **Availability**: Was a request responded to?
- **Latency**: How long did it take to respond?
- **Throughput**: How many requests can be handled?

### Data Storage Systems

- **Latency**: How long does it take to read and write data?
- **Availability**: Is the data there when needed?
- **Durability**: If there is a failure, do we lose any data?

## Key Performance Indicators (KPIs)

Business decision makers want to measure the value of projects to support the most valuable ones and avoid wasting resources. KPIs can be categorized as business KPIs and technical KPIs.

### Business KPIs

- Measure what the business values, such as ROI, earnings before interest and taxes, customer churn, and employee turnover.

### Technical KPIs

- Measure aspects like software effectiveness through page views, user registration, and number of checkouts. These should align closely with business objectives.

### Defining KPIs

- **Goal**: The outcome or result you want to achieve.
- **KPI**: A metric indicating whether you are on track to achieve the goal.
- **Targets**: Define what success looks like for each KPI.

### SMART Criteria for KPIs

- **Specific**: Clearly defined and unambiguous.
- **Measurable**: Able to be monitored to indicate progress.
- **Achievable**: Realistic and attainable.
- **Relevant**: Directly related to the goal.
- **Time-bound**: Defined within a specific timeframe.

## Service Level Terminology

### Service Level Indicators (SLIs)

- Quantitative measures of some aspect of the level of service being provided (e.g., throughput, latency, error rate).

### Service Level Objectives (SLOs)

- Agreed-upon targets or ranges of values for a service level measured by an SLI (e.g., average latency of HTTP requests should be less than 100 milliseconds).

### Service Level Agreements (SLAs)

- Agreements between a service provider and a consumer defining responsibilities for delivering a service and consequences when these responsibilities are not met. SLAs are more restrictive versions of SLOs.

### Measurable Indicators

- Indicators must be measurable and the way they are aggregated needs careful consideration (e.g., using percentiles for metrics like latency to show worst-case values).

# Relevance of SLOs and SLAs

## Service Level Objectives (SLOs)

The relevance of SLOs is vital. You want objectives that help or improve the user experience. It is easy to define SLOs based on what is easy to measure rather than what is useful. For clarity, SLOs should specify how they are measured and the conditions when they are valid.

### Key Points

- **Realistic Targets**: It is unrealistic and undesirable to have SLOs with a 100% target. Such targets result in expensive, overly conservative solutions that are still unlikely to reach the SLO. It is better to track the rate at which SLOs are missed and work to improve this.
- **Achievable Goals**: In many cases, 99% availability may be good enough and far easier to achieve and engineer. It is also likely to be more cost-effective.
- **Use Case Consideration**: The use case needs to be considered. For example, an SLO of 80% may be more achievable and good enough for a photo upload service used primarily on mobile phones.
- **Multiple SLOs**: It is often okay to specify multiple SLOs to capture different aspects of performance.

### Example

- 90% of HTTP GET calls will complete in less than 50 milliseconds.
- 99% of HTTP GET calls will complete in less than 100 milliseconds.
- 99.9% of HTTP GET calls will complete in less than 500 milliseconds.

### Tips for Selecting SLOs

- **Do Not Make Them Too High**: Start with lower SLOs and tighten them over time as you learn about the system.
- **Keep Them Simple**: More complex SLIs can obscure important changes in performance.
- **Avoid Absolute Values**: 100% availability is unrealistic and increases complexity and cost.
- **Minimize SLOs**: Have just enough SLOs to cover key system attributes.

## Service Level Agreements (SLAs)

An SLA is a business contract between the service provider and the customer. A penalty will apply if the service provider does not maintain the levels agreed on. Not every service has an SLA, but all services should have SLOs.

### Key Points

- **Conservative SLAs**: Be conservative with SLAs because it is difficult to change or remove SLAs that offer little value or cause a large amount of work.
- **Financial Implications**: Setting SLAs too high can result in unnecessary compensation being paid.
- **Buffer Between SLO and SLA**: An SLA should have a threshold that is lower than the SLO to provide protection and some level of safety.

### Example

- **Service**: HTTP endpoint accessed using HTTP GET.
- **SLI**: End-to-end latency of successful HTTP responses (HTTP-200), averaged over one minute.
- **SLO**: Latency of 99% of the responses must be less than or equal to 200 milliseconds.
- **SLA**: User is compensated if the 99th percentile latency exceeds 300 milliseconds.

The SLA has built a buffer over the SLO, providing capacity before the SLA is broken.
